Social media platforms have fundamentally changed the nature of public discourse, and not for the better. When anyone can broadcast to millions with zero editorial oversight, the inevitable result is that the loudest, most outrageous voices win attention over the most accurate or careful ones. This is not a coincidence—it is structural. Platforms optimise for engagement, and outrage drives engagement more reliably than nuance. We have the internal documents to prove it.

The consequence is a degraded epistemic commons. Citizens who rely on social media as their primary news source are systematically exposed to a more distorted picture of reality than those who read long-form journalism or books. Studies comparing news comprehension across media types consistently show this gap. If we care about having a democracy that functions on accurate information, we should care about the conditions under which citizens form their beliefs.

Therefore, some form of regulatory intervention is justified—at minimum, transparency requirements about algorithmic amplification, and ideally structural limits on the business model that monetises attention through emotional provocation.
