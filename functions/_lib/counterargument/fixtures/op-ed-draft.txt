The Democratic Case for Making Voting Compulsory

In the 2022 United States midterm elections, turnout reached a record high for a midterm cycle — and still only 47 per cent of eligible voters participated. In the 2019 United Kingdom general election, the figure was 67 per cent. These numbers are not evidence of public indifference alone; they are evidence of a systematic failure. Low voluntary turnout does not produce a random sample of citizens. It reliably overrepresents older, wealthier, and more ideologically committed voters while systematically underrepresenting younger people, renters, and those working multiple jobs. The result is that elected governments, however legitimate by procedural standards, are not chosen by a representative cross-section of the people they govern.

The solution is compulsory voting — the requirement that all eligible citizens cast a ballot, with a range of non-vote options for those who object to all candidates. Australia has operated such a system since 1924, with turnout consistently above 90 per cent. The Australian example is not merely anecdotal: research comparing cohorts of Australians born just above and below the age threshold for each federal election shows that compulsory voting increases civic participation in other domains, reduces the information advantages of parties with well-funded get-out-the-vote operations, and narrows the partisan gap that opens when only motivated partisans show up.

The objection that voting should be a free choice misunderstands the nature of the obligation. Jury duty, tax returns, and census participation are all compulsory civic obligations in most liberal democracies, and we do not typically regard them as violations of liberty. The obligation to vote is no more coercive than these requirements. Citizens who object to all available candidates retain meaningful agency: they can cast a blank ballot, rank a protest candidate, or formally mark "none of the above." What compulsory voting removes is not the substance of political choice but the free-rider problem that allows non-participation to distort outcomes for those who do engage.

Low turnout also hands structural advantages to those who have already accumulated political power. Get-out-the-vote spending, voter-ID requirements, and deliberate suppression tactics all operate at the margin — they are effective precisely because they can move the small minority of persuadable occasional voters. Remove the voluntary participation problem and most of these tactics lose their leverage. A population that votes at ninety per cent is dramatically harder to suppress or microtarget than one that votes at fifty per cent.

Critics argue that forcing disengaged citizens to the ballot box will produce a surge of uninformed votes, or that it will advantage incumbents who benefit from name recognition. These concerns deserve engagement. But the alternative — a political system in which governments are selected by a self-selected minority — has the same flaws in far more severe form. Mandatory voting does not solve every problem in democratic theory. But it makes the starting condition more representative, and that matters.
