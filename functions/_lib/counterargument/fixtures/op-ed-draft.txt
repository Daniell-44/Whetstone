Algorithm or Conversation? Why Chronological Feeds Are the Only Ethical Option

Every time you open a social media app, a machine decides what you see. That machine is optimised not for your benefit, but for engagement — a proxy for advertising revenue that correlates strongly with outrage, anxiety, and compulsive checking. The solution is obvious and achievable: platforms should be legally required to show posts in the order they were published, with no algorithmic reordering.

The harm from engagement-optimised feeds is well-documented. A 2021 internal Facebook study, later reported by the Wall Street Journal, found that its algorithm consistently amplified divisive and emotionally charged content because that content generated more interaction. Instagram's own researchers concluded that the platform was harmful to teenage girls' body image partly because the recommendation engine pushed extreme content to vulnerable users. These harms are not bugs in the system — they are the system working exactly as designed.

A chronological feed breaks this loop. When posts appear in time order, the platform cannot selectively amplify whatever produces the most compulsive engagement. A sensational post gets no more visibility than a calm one. Misinformation does not receive an algorithmic tailwind simply because it provokes strong reactions. Advertisers still reach audiences, but the mechanism is reach and frequency, not behavioural manipulation.

The objection that chronological feeds are "bad for discovery" misunderstands the purpose of a social network. A social network exists to let people communicate with people they have chosen to follow. Discovery is a separate product — and one with legitimate algorithmic uses, such as surfacing new creators users might enjoy. These are separable. Requiring chronological ordering for the main feed does not preclude a clearly labelled discovery tab. The platforms conflate these deliberately to preserve their leverage over what information reaches users.

A second objection holds that legislators lack the technical competence to regulate feed design. This is a counsel of despair dressed as a technical argument. Lawmakers do not need to understand the internals of a recommendation model to mandate that posts appear in chronological order. That requirement is trivially simple to verify by audit. Platforms implement chronological feeds already, as opt-in settings, proving the capability exists. The only question is whether the state has the will to require it.

Critics argue that users prefer algorithmic feeds because engagement data says so. But this is circular: users engage more with content chosen to maximise engagement. This tells us nothing about what users would prefer in a counterfactual world where the feed was designed with their interests in mind. Revealed preference cannot vindicate a preference that was itself manufactured by the system whose outputs we are using to measure it.

The stakes are clear. Engagement-optimised algorithms have spent fifteen years demonstrating that they are structurally incompatible with a healthy information environment. Treating this as an open policy question at this stage is not neutrality — it is a political choice in favour of the status quo. Chronological feeds are not a silver bullet. But requiring them is the minimum legislative intervention consistent with taking the documented harms seriously.
