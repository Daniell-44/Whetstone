::landscape
GenCost is the annual electricity generation cost report CSIRO produces with the Australian Energy Market Operator, now in its eighth update. Its 2024-25 edition became the evidentiary spine of the federal nuclear fight, and the 2025-26 final, released on 15 July 2026, keeps the verdict intact. This briefing takes no side on whether Australia should build nuclear. It audits the report as an argument: do the method choices that produce the nuclear ranking measure the technology fairly?

The conclusion under audit is more carefully scoped than its public life suggests. CSIRO's own summary is that "Nuclear power does not currently provide the most cost competitive solution for low emission electricity in Australia", a claim about the present, not about possibility. The current edition places the technology precisely: "Gas with CCS is the next most competitive after solar thermal and offshore wind. Large-scale nuclear is slightly higher in cost than gas with CCS. Black coal with CCS occupies a similar cost range to large-scale nuclear. Nuclear small modular reactors (SMRs) are the highest cost."

Four method choices generate that ranking, and every one is contested. Capacity factor: nuclear gets a 53 to 89 per cent range, derived, in the report's words, "on the Australian evidence, applying a maximum of 89% and minimum of 53%", with the Australian coal fleet as the reference class. Plant life: capital is recovered over 30 years because, per the report's FAQ, "It's standard practice that the financing period for an asset is less than its full operational life, similar to a car or house loan." First build: "To develop the premium the value of 120% has been applied to large scale nuclear based on Flyvbjerg and Gardner (2023)", general megaproject overrun research rather than nuclear-specific Australian data. And timing: "Many stakeholders have agreed with the GenCost estimate of at least 15 years lead time for nuclear generation", a clock that starts only once the federal legal ban is repealed, which as of the February 2026 Senate record it has not been.

::position colour=0 source=price label="Wrong frame entirely" quote="On a system-wide average economic cost basis, including nuclear remains cheaper than a renewables-only system in equilibrium and in transition."
The frontal rejection comes from the author of the rival model. Frontier Economics' December 2024 reports supplied the Coalition's nuclear costings, and when critics converged on them, managing director Danny Price defended the frame itself: "On a system-wide average economic cost basis, including nuclear remains cheaper than a renewables-only system in equilibrium and in transition." On this view GenCost's technology-by-technology comparison answers the wrong question; only whole-of-system cost across the transition counts, and on that measure his model finds for nuclear.
::audit name="Modal Overreach" kind=structural
A single model construction cannot carry a claim about every practical eventuality, and Frontier's underlying report escalates to near-certainty that nuclear inclusion could never in practice cost more. Because Frontier publishes no levelised cost or capacity factor, the construction cannot be probed from outside. The inference holds inside the model; the modality claimed extends well beyond it.

What the model finds depends on how it is built, and Frontier's own pages supply the audit trail. Its like-for-like result is real but modest: "The inclusion of nuclear power in the NEM in the Step Change scenario is 25% cheaper than AEMO’s renewables and storage approach." The 44 per cent headline that led the political campaign comes from a different construction, "a Progressive future which includes nuclear power is 44% cheaper than the Step Change future", which varies demand and technology together. The model also assumes nuclear capital at "$10,000/kW", amortises it over 50 years, and keeps most of the coal fleet open: "The delay to early closure of coal affects about 65% of current coal generation fleet in the NEM." Its strongest claim is modal: "It is very unlikely that the inclusion of nuclear power in the NEM would, in practice, ever be more expensive than AEMO’s approach." GenCost answered inside its own covers, concluding the Frontier report "cannot be used to draw any conclusions about the cost of nuclear generation relative to other generation options". [OWNER: that final quoted sentence is sourced from the research locator for GenCost Section 1.3.1, not a bank quote field; verify it against the accessible-text file before publish.]

::position colour=1 source=hilton label="Internally inconsistent" quote="The GenCost report is internally inconsistent on nuclear capacity factors. The authors admit brown coal is almost always on, achieving a 90% capacity factor, because its low fuel cost allows it to underbid other generators. But according to the CSIRO, nuclear fuel will be even cheaper than brown coal in coming decades, so the same logic should apply."
The sharpest parameter attack is an internal-consistency argument, and it needs no rival data. Zoe Hilton of the Centre for Independent Studies: "The GenCost report is internally inconsistent on nuclear capacity factors. The authors admit brown coal is almost always on, achieving a 90% capacity factor, because its low fuel cost allows it to underbid other generators. But according to the CSIRO, nuclear fuel will be even cheaper than brown coal in coming decades, so the same logic should apply." The report's own merit-order logic, turned on the report: if cheap fuel keeps brown coal running at 90 per cent, cheap-fuelled nuclear should earn the same treatment.
::audit name="Contested Warrant" kind=structural
The parity argument is valid if GenCost's capacity factors are set by dispatch economics, where cheap fuel runs first. But GenCost's stated warrant for the range is different: investor prudence across observed global nuclear outcomes. The criticism and the defence rest on different warrants and neither engages the other's. The inference holds given its premise; the premise is the dispute.

CSIRO's defence states the objection before answering it. The report renders the critics' position as: "Due to US experience, capacity factors of below 93% should not be considered (GenCost uses the range 53% to 89%)". Its reply concedes the top of the range and defends the bottom: "To be clear GenCost agrees that high capacity factors of around 90% are achievable for nuclear generation. However, a prudent investor (government or private) must prepare for all plausible eventualities. The observed global average capacity factor for nuclear generation is 80% and 10% of nuclear generation is operating at below 60%." The reference class doing the quiet work is domestic: "Australia operates a similar steam turbine based technology in coal generation for which the average capacity factor over the last decade was 59 per cent with a maximum of 89 per cent." Everything turns on whether an Australian reactor's future is better predicted by Australia's coal fleet or by the world's nuclear fleet, and that is a judgment call, not a measurement.

The pattern repeats on plant life. Critics say a 30-year recovery window roughly doubles nuclear's apparent capital charge against a machine designed to run 60. The report ran the longer life and priced the difference: "Taking account of extension costs, long operational life confers an average cost reduction of 8% to nuclear power relative to the costs that are calculated when only considering the standard 30-year private sector financial arrangements." That 8 per cent is the load-bearing number in the rebuttal, and it inherits two assumptions of its own, the cost of mid-life refurbishment and the price of rebuilding shorter-lived technologies, neither displayed in the public-facing material.

::position colour=2 source=wood label="One parameter decides it" quote="While both use similar estimates of the capital cost of nuclear plants, the big difference is that Frontier has assumed nuclear plants would run 90 per cent of the time, while CSIRO has used a range of 54-89 per cent, more akin to what coal currently achieves."
Grattan Institute's Tony Wood, adjudicating between the reports, locates the whole gap in one parameter: "While both use similar estimates of the capital cost of nuclear plants, the big difference is that Frontier has assumed nuclear plants would run 90 per cent of the time, while CSIRO has used a range of 54-89 per cent, more akin to what coal currently achieves." The concession in the first clause matters as much as the criticism in the second. If the capital-cost inputs match, the fight is not over what a reactor costs to build but over how much electricity it is assumed to sell. [OWNER: Wood says 54-89; the GenCost 2024-25 final says 53-89. Reconcile or footnote before publish.]
::audit name="Inherited Reference Class" kind=interpretive
The isolation of the load-bearing parameter is accurate and the capital-cost concession is the most useful sentence in the exchange. But "more akin to what coal currently achieves" adopts GenCost's reference class by proximity rather than argument. Whether Australian coal or the operating overseas nuclear fleet better predicts an Australian reactor is the entire dispute, and here it is settled by analogy, not evidence.

The first-of-a-kind premium shows how one parameter can be attacked from both directions at once. Its basis is general megaproject research, not nuclear data: "To develop the premium the value of 120% has been applied to large scale nuclear based on Flyvbjerg and Gardner (2023)". Advocates argue an experienced foreign vendor would not pay a full premium. The Clean Energy Council, citing IEEFA, argues the base being multiplied is already too low: "the most recent international experience indicates construction costs of between $15,000-$28,000/kWh, excluding financing costs during the construction period". [OWNER: the /kWh is the CEC document's own typo for /kW, reproduced verbatim; decide whether to sic it.] Beneath both sits a further ruling: "Large-scale nuclear is treated as a mature technology and therefore is not assigned any learning rate whereby cost reductions are achieved as a function of deployment." The two rulings are consistent, one concerns global technology maturity and the other local first-build inexperience, but the pairing means Australian nuclear takes a premium at entry and no discount over time, and each half of that is separately contested.

::position colour=3 source=quiggin label="Too generous to nuclear" quote="Even under the favorable conditions of a brownfield site and an established nuclear industry, new nuclear power is hopelessly uneconomic."
From the opposite flank, John Quiggin reads a real contract against the model. On the Czech Dukovany deal, whose contracted price came in around 50 per cent above GenCost's capital-cost estimate: "Even under the favorable conditions of a brownfield site and an established nuclear industry, new nuclear power is hopelessly uneconomic." The direction of this criticism is the audit's most useful data point. GenCost is attacked here for flattering nuclear, by an economist broadly friendly to the transition, at the same time as it is attacked elsewhere for handicapping it.
::audit name="Hasty Generalisation" kind=empirical
One contracted price is strong one-directional evidence: a real deal exceeded the estimate by half under favourable conditions, so the estimate is not padded against nuclear. It is weak evidence for "hopelessly uneconomic", which needs a distribution of outcomes, not a point. As a check on which way GenCost's errors lean, the datum is fair; the adjective outruns it.

Then the measure itself moved. The 2025-26 edition introduced a system-wide levelised cost, SLCOE, with a one-line concession attached: "LCOE does not include renewable integration costs. SLCOE does." It published the system model open source. And when consultation pushed on how the new measure counts the existing fleet, it retreated in the open: "In response to this feedback, for 2030 SLCOE estimates have been replaced with electricity futures prices as an alternative indicator of generation prices in the period to 2030." Outside readers scored the change. Clayton Utz called SLCOE "a material refinement of the GenCost framework, and an acknowledgement that generation technologies cannot be assessed meaningfully in isolation from their system impacts". The critics did not accept settlement: the CIS submission argues the new method still "robbed the public and policymakers of information". For this audit the sequence matters more than the scoring. The frame the 2024-25 edition defended is the frame the 2025-26 edition amended.

::position colour=4 source=mountain label="The question overclaims" quote="Comparing the average costs of wind, solar, nuclear, hydro and coal power sounds like a simple thing to do. But the only plausible answer is: ‘it depends on many barely known and many unknown things’."
The last position refuses the contest's terms. Bruce Mountain, an energy economist supportive of the transition yet a GenCost critic, argues the defect is not in any parameter but in the genre: "Comparing the average costs of wind, solar, nuclear, hydro and coal power sounds like a simple thing to do. But the only plausible answer is: ‘it depends on many barely known and many unknown things’." On this reading, GenCost's sin and Frontier's sin are the same sin. Both present assumption-driven outputs with a certainty the underlying exercise cannot support.
::audit name="The Inference Holds" kind=interpretive
As an audit of certainty claims this briefing can largely endorse it: the outputs demonstrably move with the assumptions, and no party's headline carries that sensitivity. What it does not do is adjudicate any specific parameter. Wide error bars argue for publishing sensitivities, not for treating all rankings as equally likely, and planners still have to choose under the uncertainty it describes.

::shared
Strip the camps away and one assumption stands under all of them: that a cost model, correctly parameterised, is the instrument that settles the question. GenCost itself concedes the outputs are revisable, "In any given year, we are readily able to change assumptions in response to stakeholder input.", Frontier's savings shrink from 44 to 25 per cent under a different scenario pairing, and every actor plotted above fights over parameters rather than over the frame's authority. The parties also share more ground than the headlines suggest: nobody in the costing fight disputes that coal is exiting and a transition is underway, and Grattan notes both reports feed in similar nuclear capital costs per kilowatt. The contest runs entirely inside an apparatus whose outputs, all sides agree when pressed, move with the hand that sets the assumptions.

::editor
The report survives its audit better than its loudest critics claim and worse than its defenders assume. Each contested choice has a stated warrant applied by consistent formula rather than improvised per technology, and the strongest evidence of good faith is behavioural: when criticism landed, CSIRO changed its measure, open-sourced the model, and withdrew a number it could not defend. But the two parameters doing the most work in the nuclear ranking, the coal-fleet reference class and the 30-year recovery window, are conventions defended by analogy, and no edition's headline carries the assumption sensitivity the report itself concedes. ::why-wrong
The behavioural read may be too generous: amending the measure in the edition after the election was decided conceded nothing while the concession mattered. Or the evenhandedness flatters the critics: if the observed record, a global average capacity factor of 80 per cent and a contracted price 50 per cent above estimate, is the right prior for a first-mover build, then GenCost's choices are not cautious conventions but the empirically grounded position.
