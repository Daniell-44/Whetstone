::landscape
In late 2024 the consultancy Frontier Economics published a pair of reports modelling the cost of adding nuclear power to the National Electricity Market: a base-case report on 14 November, and on 13 December the analysis that produced the number Australian politics then argued about for a year, that a nuclear-inclusive pathway would cost $331 billion against $594 billion for the pathway in AEMO's Integrated System Plan, a gap the report states as 44%. The firm describes the work as self-funded and self-directed; it was the Federal Coalition that carried the result into an election year as its central energy cost claim. This piece takes no side on whether Australia should build nuclear plants. It audits the report as an argument: what is claimed, on what warrant, from which assumptions, and whether the method choices support the headline.

Start with the report's own disclosures, because they frame everything downstream. Frontier states that "The work undertaken for this report is funded and directed solely by Frontier Economics." Three sentences earlier it also states: "We have consulted with the Federal Coalition through the course of this work to determine more details about their plans to help clarify how we could model the inclusion of nuclear power in the NEM." These are not contradictory, but they sit in tension once you see where the consultation lands: the report's decisive analytical parameter, which demand scenario the nuclear pathway is costed on, is justified on the ground that the Coalition "confirmed that they consider the demand forecast embodied in the Progressive scenario is more consistent with their view of the most likely transition of the electricity market". Independence of funding is not independence of framing. The parameter that generates the headline tracks the sponsor-adjacent party's stated worldview, not a modelling result.

The modelling itself produces a two-by-two grid, scenario by technology, and all four cells are in the report. Step Change without nuclear: $594 billion. Step Change with nuclear: $446 billion. Progressive without nuclear: $437 billion. Progressive with nuclear: $331 billion. Frontier's own text gives the like-for-like results: "The inclusion of nuclear power in the NEM in the Step Change scenario is 25% cheaper than AEMO's renewables and storage approach", and within Progressive it is "also 25% cheaper". The 44% headline is the diagonal of the grid: it changes the demand scenario and the technology at the same time. Holding technology fixed and changing only the scenario moves the no-nuclear system from $594 billion to $437 billion, so $157 billion of the $263 billion headline saving arrives before a single reactor enters the model.

The nuclear cells themselves rest on a small set of inputs, all stated in Section 1.4: "Capital costs are $10,000 per kilowatt of capacity", improving at 1% a year; $30 per megawatt hour covering all variable and non-capital fixed costs including decommissioning; first power in 2036 with roughly 13,000 MW commissioned by 2049; and a coal bridge to get there: "In the report modelling, some existing coal fired generators in the NEM are maintained beyond AEMO's early closure dates to ensure that there is sufficient generation capacity to meet the system reliability requirements. The delay to early closure of coal affects about 65% of current coal generation fleet in the NEM." No refurbishment, subsidy or reliability cost for that extension is modelled.

::position colour=0 source=frontier label="The system-cost case for 44%" quote="As argued in Report 1, a more appropriate basis of comparison is the total cost of a power system – generators, storages, and transmission – to reliably and securely meet demand."
The report's argument runs in two steps. Step one is a framing move against levelised-cost comparisons of the GenCost kind, which it calls crude and misleading: "As argued in Report 1, a more appropriate basis of comparison is the total cost of a power system – generators, storages, and transmission – to reliably and securely meet demand." Step two re-optimises both AEMO demand scenarios with nuclear forced in and headlines the cross-scenario cell: Progressive with nuclear at $331 billion against Step Change without it at $594 billion, "44% cheaper" in the report's words. The scenario crossing is defended twice over: the Coalition confirmed Progressive matches its view of the transition, and AEMO itself rates Progressive at 42% likelihood against 43% for Step Change. The report adds a robustness claim: assumed nuclear costs "could double on a sustained basis" before the ranking flipped.
::audit name="Equivocation" kind=structural
On the report's own like-for-like cells nuclear saves 25% in either scenario; the 44% exists only when demand and technology change together, so "cheaper" slides between doing the same job for less and doing a smaller job for less. The report discloses the construction in Section 1.5.1, and the slide completes in the firm's own compression, "In both scenarios, including nuclear power in our energy mix is cheaper – by up to 44% - for Australians in the medium-term future", a sentence whose figure appears in neither scenario alone.

Everything the critics say starts from those four cells. The most sympathetic reading in the debate comes from the industry body with no technology to sell in this fight.

::position colour=1 source=aec label="Different metrics, not contradictions" quote="Frontier have used a seven per cent real weighted average cost of capital for discounting. Frontier argues consumers don’t pay the NPV, they pay the real costs. The report also comments that the concept of NPV is confusing and people would prefer to understand the total costs."
The Australian Energy Council reads the loudest fights over the report as narrower than they sound. On the choice to headline undiscounted totals: "Frontier have used a seven per cent real weighted average cost of capital for discounting. Frontier argues consumers don’t pay the NPV, they pay the real costs. The report also comments that the concept of NPV is confusing and people would prefer to understand the total costs." And on the apparent collision between Frontier's $594 billion and the government's $122 billion figure, the AEC's explanation is deflationary: "A few more areas noted in the Frontier work is that the quoted $122 billion NPV costs are only capital costs, so do not include Fixed Operations and Maintenance (FOM), Variable Operations and Maintenance (VOM) expenses associated with running and maintaining plant, or fuel and emissions costs." Different metrics, not different facts.
::audit name="No Structural Fault" kind=interpretive
The disambiguation holds, and it cuts both ways: Frontier's own NPV table gives $225 billion against $124 billion and states "The relative costs between the scenarios are the same as for the sum real costs", so the discounting dispute is about presentation, not arithmetic. What remains is a documentation defect rather than a fallacy: Report 2's only pointer to its discount rate reads "This uses the WACC value specified in Section Error! Reference source not found..", and the 7% figure has to be recovered from Report 1.

The measured middle of the criticism grants the system-cost framing and attacks two parameters.

::position colour=2 source=grattan label="Same scenario, smaller saving" quote="Both plans should be tested against the same economic growth scenarios, in which case the savings from nuclear fall by as much as $150bn."
Tony Wood at Grattan concedes more to the report than most critics: "While both use similar estimates of the capital cost of nuclear plants, the big difference is that Frontier has assumed nuclear plants would run 90 per cent of the time, while CSIRO has used a range of 54-89 per cent, more akin to what coal currently achieves." His structural objection is the scenario crossing, and he prices it: "Both plans should be tested against the same economic growth scenarios, in which case the savings from nuclear fall by as much as $150bn." On Frontier's own grid, that is roughly the distance between the 44% headline and the 25% like-for-like result.
::audit name="Unstated Ceteris-Paribus Warrant" kind=structural
Wood's test assumes a cost comparison must hold demand fixed and vary only the technology, which is exactly the warrant Frontier rejects when it costs the Coalition pathway on the Coalition's demand outlook. Wood's is the stronger form of the objection because the warrant is explicit, and granting it, his arithmetic follows from Frontier's published cells; but the argument does not itself establish that the warrant is mandatory rather than one convention among two.

::position colour=3 source=ieefa label="Not like-for-like, and inputs too low" quote="The Coalition Opposition headline figures compare the cost of the market operator’s plan to serve a larger electricity consumption, with the cost for the nuclear plan to serve a smaller electricity consumption. However, these are not like-for-like."
IEEFA's Johanna Bowyer states the demand objection at the headline: "The Coalition Opposition headline figures compare the cost of the market operator’s plan to serve a larger electricity consumption, with the cost for the nuclear plan to serve a smaller electricity consumption. However, these are not like-for-like." She then moves down a level, to the inputs: "The modelling uses a $10 billion per gigawatt (GW) cost for large-scale nuclear reactor construction. However recent international experience shows actual construction costs of $15 billion to $28 billion per GW excluding financing costs. This is 1.5 to almost three times the Coalition Opposition figure." And to the comparison window: with capital spread over 50 years from a 2036 start, "This means that the Coalition’s proposal appears to have a long tail of cost outside of the modelled period that consumers will continue having to pay for."
::audit name="Contested Reference Class" kind=empirical
The capex inference is sound if recent Western first-builds are the right comparator for a first Australian program, and it matters because the observed range consumes the report's stated buffer that assumed costs "could double on a sustained basis" before parity was lost. Price's reply is that far cheaper recent projects exist, but he names none in the op-ed, so on the published record IEEFA's premise is the better evidenced; the inference holds exactly as far as the reference class does.

The flat rejection comes from the peak body whose members build the competing system, an interest worth weighing alongside the argument itself.

::position colour=4 source=cec label="An impossible timeline" quote="An assumption that nuclear power plants can be built and operational starting from 2036. They can't – making the Frontier/Coalition's Nuclear Alternative scenario an impossible and misleading piece of modelling and analysis."
The Clean Energy Council's briefing note rejects the exercise outright, leading with the timeline: "An assumption that nuclear power plants can be built and operational starting from 2036. They can't – making the Frontier/Coalition's Nuclear Alternative scenario an impossible and misleading piece of modelling and analysis." Its version of the scenario objection is the most mechanical statement of it in the debate: Frontier costs the nuclear pathway on "the Progressive Change electricity demand profile, which represents 26 per cent lower electricity demand (operational demand) than AEMO's Step Change scenario", and "Lower demand requires a smaller system build, and a lower capital spend."
::audit name="Modal Overreach" kind=structural
"They can't" asserts impossibility where the evidence supports strong improbability: CSIRO's finding that "at least 15 years remains the most plausible lead time" makes first power in 2036, about eleven years from the report's release, very unlikely rather than logically impossible, and the ban was still unrepealed as of February 2026. The conclusion survives in weakened modal form; the note claims more than its warrant delivers.

One further feature of the report deserves naming because it is an argument move, not a modelling one. Report 2 states of the cost classes it excluded from both sides of its own comparison: "These missing costs will be very large and when they are taken into account the total cost of the transition of the electricity sector will be well above a trillion dollars over the next 25-30 years if Australia continues with AEMO's transition plans." The exclusions are symmetric; the un-modelled trillion is attached only to AEMO's pathway. An asymmetric aside of that kind is an advocacy marker inside a modelling document, whatever one concludes about the model.

A currency note, because this audit is written in July 2026 about a December 2024 document. AEMO published its 2026 Integrated System Plan on 25 June 2026, superseding the 2024 ISP both Frontier reports model against, and the GenCost cycle has run on with its nuclear verdict unchanged in direction. Politically, the Coalition lost the May 2025 election, and its operative nuclear commitment has narrowed to lifting the federal ban, which remained in force as of the adjourned Senate debate of 4 February 2026; the seven-reactor build program the report costed appears in no post-election policy statement. No updated Frontier costing was found in the preparation of this piece. ::shared
Beneath the fight, the report and its critics stand on shared ground. All parties accept that the coal fleet is exiting and that the argument is over its replacement, not over whether a transition happens; nearly all now argue in the currency Frontier insisted on, whole-of-system cost rather than generator-level comparison, a frame CSIRO's own later GenCost work moved toward; all treat AEMO's scenario suite as the legitimate menu of futures, disputing only which scenario may be used and by whom; and every number in the dispute is the output of a long-run model that tracks its assumptions, which is why each side can be arithmetically correct inside its own frame while the frames disagree. The certainty on display, on both sides, exceeds what modelling of this kind can deliver.

::editor
Read structurally, the report proves less than its headline and more than its harshest critics allow. The within-scenario result, that nuclear saves 25% on Frontier's stated assumptions, is a genuine modelling finding whose fate turns on contested empirical inputs: the capital cost, the operating cost, the capacity factor, the timeline and the free coal bridge. The 44% figure is a different object, produced by changing the demand scenario and the technology at once and letting the word "cheaper" carry the like-for-like reading it does not have. ::why-wrong
Frontier's defence of the diagonal is not empty: costing a party's policy on that party's stated demand outlook is a recognisable form of scenario analysis, and AEMO itself rated Progressive only one point less likely than Step Change. If that warrant is accepted, the 44% is disclosed advocacy rather than sleight of hand, and the audit's weight shifts entirely to the empirical inputs, where the report could yet be vindicated if builds at or below its assumed costs materialise.
